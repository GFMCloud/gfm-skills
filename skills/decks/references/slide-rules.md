# Slide rules

Rules for planning, building and reviewing slides. Each rule names the usual fix.

## Contents

- Planning: one job per slide
- Reading: does the slide read?
- Charts and numbers
- Review output

## Planning: one job per slide

1. **Headlines are claims.** "Support tickets fell 30% after the new help page", not "Support
   update". A reader who sees only the headlines should get the argument.
2. **One job per slide.** If a slide makes two points, it is two slides. Most crowded slides are
   this problem, not a styling one.
3. **Length has a point of view.** A presented deck that lands is usually 8 to 15 slides for 10 to
   20 minutes. Merge slides that make the same point; say so when an outline is bloated.
4. **Every slide in a plan has five fields:** the headline (claim), the job (what the audience should
   think or do after it), the evidence (fact, number or example, with its source), the visual
   (slide type from the template), and the speaker note.
5. **No invented facts.** Where the material doesn't say it, write `[NEEDS YOUR INPUT: what is
   missing]` instead of a plausible number, customer or quote, and list every marker at the end.
6. **Lead with the answer.** Put the recommendation or result early; the evidence follows it.

## Reading: does the slide read?

7. **One dominant element.** In half a second the eye should land on one thing: the headline, the big
   number or the hero bar. If everything has the same weight, nothing leads.
8. **Two type sizes.** Headline and body. Use weight, not a third or fourth size, for anything else.
9. **Flush left.** Centre only a title, a statement or a quote. Never justify.
10. **White space is doing work.** Cut words before shrinking margins or type. Five bullets at most;
    three is better.
11. **Colour means something.** One accent per slide, on the thing that matters; everything else
    neutral. Brand colours come from the brand lookup, not from taste.
12. **No decoration.** Remove shadows, gradient blobs, boxes inside boxes, an accent line under every
    title, icons that don't inform, and fake device frames. If deleting it loses nothing, it was noise.
13. **A steady grid.** Margins and positions stay the same from slide to slide, so the audience reads
    content, not a new layout each time.

## Charts and numbers

14. **Pick the chart for the question.** Change over time: line. Comparing categories: bar (bars start
    at zero). Part of a whole with two or three parts: a single stacked bar or just the numbers. No
    pie charts, no dual y-axes, no 3D. More than five lines: small multiples.
15. **One hero, the rest grey.** The series or bar the slide is about gets the accent.
16. **Annotate like a sentence.** The headline states the takeaway; a line below names the unit and
    period; values sit on the bars or line ends, not in a legend.
17. **Every number has a source you can re-read.** A file, a query or the user's own figure. Put a
    source line on the slide. Never plot a remembered or rounded-from-memory number; demo data is
    labelled as demo on the slide.
18. **Say what the picture leaves out:** partial periods, excluded rows, a sample of one, a truncated
    axis on anything that isn't a bar.

## Review output

Lead with the one or two problems that repeat across the deck, then go slide by slide. For each
problem: the slide number, the element, the rule, and the concrete fix ("cut bullets 4 to 6 and move
them to the appendix", not "too busy"). Say "reads well" for slides that pass, and don't invent
problems.
