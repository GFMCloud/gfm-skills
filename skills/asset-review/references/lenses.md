# The two lenses

Read the whole asset first. Then apply the buyer lens to every asset, and the field lens whenever a
rep, partner or account team carries the asset into a conversation. Weight the checks by what the
asset is for; a check that doesn't apply to this asset type is skipped, not failed.

## Which lens leads, by asset type

| Asset | Lead lens | Weigh most |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Sell sheet, one-pager, solution brief | both | entry point, proof next to claims, advisor or catalogue |
| Outbound email or sequence | buyer | the first line, one problem, one ask, length |
| Case study | buyer | the customer's problem before the solution, a number with a timeframe, their words |
| Talk track, call script, battlecard | field | opening question, the question sequence, objections, where it lands |
| Deck content (what the slides say) | both | the story across headlines, entry point, the ask on the last slide |
| Landing page or web copy | buyer | the headline names the reader's problem, proof near each claim, one call to action |
| Proposal or statement-of-work summary | buyer | their problem in their words, the outcome, the next step, room to grow |
| Internal enablement (launch note to sales) | field | what a rep says first, to whom, and what not to say |

## Buyer lens: will the reader act on it?

Read it as the named reader would, with their day, their boss and three other vendors in mind.

- **Their problem first.** The reader's problem, in their words, comes before the offer. An asset
  that opens with the seller ("We are a leading provider...") makes the reader do the translating.
- **So what.** For each claim ask what changes for this reader. A feature with no consequence is
  noise to them.
- **Swap test.** Put a competitor's name in place of the seller's. Every sentence that is still true
  is not a reason to choose this seller.
- **Proof next to the claim.** A named customer, a number with a timeframe and a baseline, or a
  quote. Adjectives are not proof. A claim with no proof is either cut or marked as needing it.
- **One next step.** One ask, sized to where the reader is (a cold reader won't book a two-hour
  workshop). Several competing calls to action is no call to action.
- **Their vocabulary.** Internal product codes, programme names and acronyms the reader doesn't use.
- **Length for the moment.** A cold email's first line has to earn the second. A proposal can be
  long if every section answers a question the buyer will ask.
- **Brand guidelines**, when the `Brand:` step found a `guidelines.md`: banned claims, required
  terms, the voice. Quote the guideline you apply.

## Field lens: can a rep sell with it?

The stance of a sales leader who has carried a number: material is good if a rep can walk into a
room with it and come out with a next meeting, a scoped piece of work or a deal. Looking good in a
review is not the test.

- **Proactive, not reactive.** Deals that arrive as a request for proposal were mostly decided
  before the seller heard of them; the margin and the leverage are already gone. Good material lets
  a rep start a conversation the buyer wasn't having yet. Reactive framing waits to be asked
  ("let us know if...", "if you're considering...", "we're here to support your initiative").
- **A sharp entry point.** One specific problem a rep can open with, specific enough that the buyer
  recognises it in their own estate. "We do cloud security" is a category, not an entry point.
- **Peer pattern.** "What we're seeing at companies like yours is..." opens without asking the buyer
  to admit a problem, and implies the seller has done this before. It is only honest with real
  evidence behind it; without it, mark the gap.
- **Discovery, not a pitch.** The material gives the rep questions in an order: where they are today,
  the pattern others hit, the capability that answers it, and where the conversation should land.
  If the seller resells other vendors, a question about one of those vendors is a second way in.
- **Advisor, not catalogue.** Lists of products, logos, licences and service names make the seller
  look like a catalogue to be price-shopped. Problems solved make them look like an advisor.
- **Worth the call time.** Leading with commodity resale or easily price-shopped work trains buyers
  to treat the seller as a catalogue. Judge margin only from what the asset or the user says; if
  you don't know, say so rather than guessing.
- **A path to more.** Does the entry point lead to a larger engagement (an assessment, then a
  programme)? A one-off transaction with nowhere to go is hard for a rep to prioritise.

## Rewrite patterns

Write rewrites out in full, ready to paste. Every fact in a rewrite comes from the asset or the
user's material; where one is missing, put a `[NEEDS: ...]` marker that says what kind of fact.

- **Reactive to proactive.** "Let us know if you need help with your cost review" becomes an opener
  that names what the seller keeps finding: "Most [peer group] we work with find [specific problem]
  in [specific place]. How are you tracking that today?" with `[NEEDS: the peer group and the
  pattern, from real accounts]` if the asset doesn't supply them.
- **Capability to problem.** "We offer A, B and C" becomes "[Problem] costs [reader] [consequence].
  We fix it by [the one capability that matters]."
- **Adjective to proof.** "Industry-leading response times" becomes the number and its source, or
  `[NEEDS: measured response time and where it comes from]`.
- **Question ladder** for talk tracks: current state, peer pattern, capability, (if reselling) the
  vendor angle, and the next step the rep proposes.
- **One ask.** Several calls to action become the one that fits the reader's stage.

## Words that usually signal a problem

Leads to look at, not verdicts: quote the line and say why it fails with this reader.

- Seller-first openers: "we are a leading", "founded in", "our mission".
- Empty adjectives: "best-in-class", "world-class", "seamless", "end-to-end", "cutting-edge",
  "robust", "holistic", "solutions" with no noun before it.
- Reactive phrases: "let us know", "reach out if", "if you're considering", "contact us to learn
  more", "we're here to help".
- Catalogue signals: bullet lists of product names, partner tiers or logos with no problem attached;
  "competitive pricing" as a lead benefit.
